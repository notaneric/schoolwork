The six big ideas

  • Bond polarity: subtract the electronegativities. A difference of 0 to 0.4 is a nonpolar bond (C-H is 0.4, so it counts as nonpolar). Above 0.4, up to 2.0, the bond is polar, and above 2.0 it is ionic. The more electronegative atom pulls the electrons and gets the partial negative charge (δ−).
  • Memorize NClOF: F 4.0, O 3.5, N 3.0, Cl 3.0. Also C 2.5 and H 2.1. Shortcut: a bond from an NClOF atom to C, H, S or P is polar. Between two NClOF atoms, subtract: N-Cl is 0.0 (nonpolar), Cl-O is 0.5 (polar).
  • Molecular polarity = polar bonds + shape. Each polar bond is an arrow (a dipole). If the arrows cancel, the molecule is nonpolar even though its bonds are polar: CO2 and CCl4.
  • The arrows cancel when every outer atom is the same and the central atom has no lone pairs (or its lone pairs sit symmetrically, as in square planar XeF4 and linear XeF2). They do not cancel when a lone pair on the central atom bends the shape (H2O, NH3, SO2) or when the outer atoms differ (CH2O, CH2Cl2).
  • Intermolecular forces (IMFs) are attractions between separate molecules. Bonds hold atoms together inside one molecule. IMFs are much weaker, and boiling a liquid overcomes its IMFs without breaking any bonds.
  • The three IMFs on Handout 14: dispersion (every molecule has it, and it grows with the number of electrons), dipole-dipole (polar molecules), and hydrogen bonding (H bonded directly to N, O or F). Stronger IMFs mean a higher boiling point.

Dominant IMF in three questions

  1. Is an H bonded directly to N, O or F? Then hydrogen bonding. H2O, NH3, HF, CH3OH.
  2. If not, is the molecule polar? Then dipole-dipole. HCl, SO2, PCl3, CH2O, IF3.
  3. If not, dispersion only. CH4, CO2, CCl4, I2, N2.

Dispersion is present in every molecule. "Dominant" here means the strongest type of IMF the molecule has, the way Handout 14 asks it. The classic trap is CH2O: it has H and it has O, but the H is bonded to C, so CH2O molecules cannot hydrogen-bond to each other.

Boiling-point trends

  • Stronger IMFs, higher boiling point. For molecules of similar size: hydrogen bonding > dipole-dipole > dispersion.
  • Same size, different IMF: CH4 (16 g/mol) boils at −162 °C, H2O (18 g/mol) at 100 °C. Ethanol (78 °C) and dimethyl ether (−24 °C) share the formula C2H6O, but only ethanol has an O-H.
  • More electrons, more dispersion: boiling points rise from He to Xe. At room temperature F2 and Cl2 are gases, Br2 is a liquid and I2 is a solid.
  • Shape counts too: a long chain touches its neighbors along more of its length than a compact ball does. Pentane (chain) boils at 36 °C and neopentane (ball) at 9.5 °C, both C5H12.

Handout 14, problem 2, worked: iodine trifluoride

  • Bond type: covalent (two nonmetals). Formula: IF3.
  • Lewis structure: 7 + 3(7) = 28 valence electrons. I in the center, single bonds to three F, three lone pairs on each F, and the last 4 electrons as 2 lone pairs on I. I is in row 5, so it can hold more than an octet.
  • SN = 3 bonds + 2 lone pairs = 5. Electron geometry: trigonal bipyramidal. The lone pairs take two equatorial spots, so the molecular geometry is T-shaped, angles under 90°.
  • Polarity: polar. The I-F bonds are polar (F is NClOF) and a T does not cancel.
  • Dominant IMF: dipole-dipole. Polar, with no H at all.
